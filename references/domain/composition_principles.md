# Composition Principles — Domain Reference

## Rule of Thirds

**Principle:** Divide the frame into a 3×3 grid. Place key elements along the lines or at their intersections.

**Application:**
- Place the subject's eyes at an upper intersection for portraits
- Position horizon lines on the upper or lower third, not center
- Use multiple power points (intersections) to balance multiple subjects

**Variations:**
- Golden Ratio (Phi grid): More organic 1:1.618 ratio
- Golden Triangle: Divide diagonally, repeat with perpendiculars

**Sources:**
- Freeman, M. (2007). The Photographer's Eye. ISBN 978-0240809342 (Tier 2)
- Block, B. (2008). The Visual Story. ISBN 978-0240807799 (Tier 2)

## Leading Lines

**Principle:** Use natural or constructed lines to guide the viewer's eye through the composition.

**Types:**
- Converging lines: Roads, rivers, fences that draw toward a vanishing point
- Diagonal lines: Create dynamism and depth
- Curved lines: S-curves (rivers, paths) create gentle flow
- Implied lines: Gaze direction, gesture, and color gradients

**Application:**
- Position camera so lines converge on the main subject
- Use leading lines to create depth (foreground → midground → background)
- Avoid lines that lead out of frame without connecting to subject

---

## Depth Layering

**Principle:** Compose with distinct foreground, midground, and background planes.

**Application:**
- Foreground: Anchor elements that frame or contextualize (2-5m from camera)
- Midground: Primary subject area (5-20m)
- Background: Environmental context, atmosphere (20m+)

**Tools in photo mode:**
- Adjust aperture (DOF) to separate planes
- Use fog/atmosphere sliders to enhance depth cues
- Position elements at different distances to create parallax

**Sources:**
- Bordwell, D., & Thompson, K. (2017). Film Art. ISBN 978-1259544627 (Tier 2)

## Balance & Visual Weight

**Principle:** Distribute visual elements to achieve equilibrium (symmetrical or asymmetrical).

**Factors affecting visual weight:**
- Size: Larger elements carry more weight
- Position: Elements further from center have more apparent weight
- Color: Warm colors (red, orange) appear heavier than cool colors
- Contrast: High-contrast elements draw attention
- Subject matter: Human faces, eyes, and text carry significant weight

**Application:**
- Asymmetrical balance: Use smaller, high-contrast elements to balance larger, low-contrast ones
- Symmetrical balance: Mirror elements across center axis (conveys formality, stability)
- Radial balance: Elements radiate from central point

---

## Framing Within the Frame

**Principle:** Use elements in the scene to create a frame around the subject.

**Types:**
- Natural frames: Doorways, windows, arches, tree branches
- Environmental frames: Light shafts, fog, rock formations
- Compositional frames: Using foreground elements to create edges

**Application:**
- Look for frames that provide context (architectural elements suggest setting)
- Use frames to create depth (foreground frame → subject → background)
- Avoid cluttered frames that distract from the main subject

---

## Negative Space

**Principle:** Deliberately leave empty areas to emphasize the subject or create mood.

**Applications:**
- Isolation: Large negative space around subject emphasizes importance
- Mood: Extensive negative space creates loneliness, contemplation
- Directional: Negative space in the direction of subject's gaze/action creates momentum

**Guidelines:**
- Use negative space to balance busy compositions
- Consider emotional impact: emptiness vs. spaciousness
- Apply 1:3 or 1:4 subject-to-space ratios for dramatic effect

---

## Symmetry & Pattern

**Principle:** Use repetition and reflection to create visual harmony.

**Types:**
- Reflection symmetry: Mirror images across an axis (water, glass)
- Rotational symmetry: Elements repeated around a point
- Pattern repetition: Textures, architectural elements, crowds

**Application:**
- Break perfect symmetry with one element for visual interest
- Use symmetry for formal, stable compositions
- Create rhythm with repeated patterns (fence posts, windows)

---

## Perspective & Foreshortening

**Principle:** Use camera angle and focal length to manipulate perceived depth.

**Foreshortening:**
- Wide angle (< 35mm): Expands depth, exaggerates perspective
- Normal (~50mm): Natural perspective, minimal distortion
- Telephoto (> 85mm): Compresses depth, flattens perspective

**Application:**
- Use wide focal length to emphasize foreground-background distance
- Use telephoto to isolate subject and compress scene layers
- Consider subject distance: Closer positioning increases perspective distortion

---

## Color as Composition

**Principle:** Use color relationships to structure the frame.

**Color harmonies:**
- Complementary: Teal-orange, red-green (high contrast, cinematic)
- Analogous: Adjacent colors on color wheel (harmonious)
- Triadic: Three evenly spaced colors (balanced, vibrant)
- Monochromatic: Single hue variations (unified, moody)

**Application:**
- Use complementary colors to separate subject from background
- Apply color grading strategically to emphasize compositional elements
- Consider color temperature: Warm vs. cool for subject-background distinction

**Sources:**
- Itten, J. (1970). The Elements of Color. ISBN 978-0471289278 (Tier 2)

---

## Eye Line & Screen Direction

**Principle:** Maintain consistent spatial relationships for continuity and viewer orientation.

**Eye line:**
- Leave headroom above subject's eyes
- Position subject's gaze to create directional interest
- Use off-center gaze to create negative space

**Screen direction (180° line):**
- Maintain consistent left-right relationship across shots
- Cross the 180° line only with clear visual motivation
- Use neutral shots to reset spatial relationships

**Sources:**
- Ward, P. (2003). Picture Composition for Film and Television. ISBN 978-0240516813 (Tier 2)
